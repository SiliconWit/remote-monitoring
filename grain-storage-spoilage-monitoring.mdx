Grain in a silo is alive. It respires slowly, and so does anything growing in it, and respiration has a signature: it consumes oxygen, releases carbon dioxide, and gives off a little heat. Storage practice has traditionally hunted the heat, because a thermometer on a cable is cheap and obvious. The heat is the weakest of the three signals and the last to arrive. #Agriculture #RemoteMonitoring #PostHarvest

## The installation

A silo holding stored maize, reading temperature and humidity inside the heap, vent position, the carbon dioxide concentration in the space between the grains, and the weather outside.

The owner's concern is specific and correct: warm damp pockets are where spoilage starts, and you cannot see them from outside the silo.

## The temperature chart, which is about the weather

<InArticleAd />


![Inside temperature and humidity across seventy one days, both moving continuously with the seasons and the daily cycle, temperature ranging between about seventeen and twenty eight degrees](/device-plots/grain-storage-silo-co2-1.png)

Inside temperature and humidity, across the season. Both move constantly. Temperature ranged from **16.8 °C to 27.9 °C** across the whole window, and it visits most of that range in any warm week.

That movement is the problem, not the signal. A silo is a large insulated mass sitting in the weather, so its temperature is dominated by what the weather is doing. To read spoilage from this chart you would have to notice a fraction of a degree of biological heat inside a swing of eleven degrees driven by the sun. Nobody can, and no threshold can either: set one low enough to catch respiration and it fires on every warm afternoon.

Humidity is more informative, because moisture is what starts the process. It is still an input rather than an outcome. It tells you that conditions are permissive. It does not tell you whether anything has actually begun.

## The carbon dioxide chart, which is about the grain

<InArticleAd />


![Carbon dioxide across the same seventy one days, sitting near four hundred parts per million for extended periods and rising into distinct episodes that reach well over two thousand](/device-plots/grain-storage-silo-co2-2.png)

Same silo, same season, the air between the grains.

Baseline sits at about **410 ppm**, which is roughly outdoor air, and that is exactly what quiet grain should read. Then there are episodes. The largest reached **2,350 ppm**:

$$\frac{2{,}350}{410} = 5.7\times \text{ the baseline}$$

and across the window the reading spent

$$46\%\ \text{of all windows above } 1{,}000\ \text{ppm}$$

Take the five days around that peak and put the three channels side by side:

$$\text{CO}_2: 380 \rightarrow 2{,}350\ \text{ppm} \quad (6.2\times)$$

$$\text{temperature}: 17.0 \rightarrow 26.7\ ^\circ\text{C} \quad \text{(inside its ordinary range)}$$

$$\text{humidity}: 40.5 \rightarrow 74.5\%$$

The temperature excursion looks dramatic until you notice that 26.7 °C is below the 27.9 °C this silo reached anyway during ordinary warm weather. Nothing about the temperature reading is out of character. The CO2 reading is six times its own baseline, and there is no weather that does that to a carbon dioxide concentration.

## Why CO2 is the leading indicator

<InArticleAd />


![Panel (a): dry grain respiring slowly, where carbon dioxide production is low and the concentration between grains sits near outdoor air. Panel (b): a wet pocket where mould is active, where respiration produces carbon dioxide many times faster while the heat released is small compared with the daily swing driven by the weather outside](./images/respiration-versus-weather.svg)

Respiration produces CO2 and heat together, but the two signals face very different competition:

- The CO2 in a silo has **essentially one source**, which is respiration. Background is outdoor air at around 400 ppm and nothing else moves it. A rise is biological by elimination.
- The heat from respiration has to be **detected inside a much larger thermal signal** driven by the sun and the season, and it is small.

So the same event produces a CO2 change of several hundred percent and a temperature change of a fraction of a degree buried in eleven degrees of weather. This is why published work on stored grain finds CO2 gives a faster and more sensitive indication of spoilage and mycotoxin risk than temperature and relative humidity, and why a wet pocket introduced deliberately raises CO2 sharply while temperature stays comparatively stable.

The consequence is not only spoiled grain. The moulds that thrive in damp maize include the ones that produce aflatoxin, which is a food safety and human health problem rather than a yield problem, and which travels with the crop into whatever it becomes.

## What this is worth

<InArticleAd />


The numbers below are a worked example, not a promise. Put your own figures in.

The economics of stored grain are unusual because the loss is not gradual. A consignment is sound, or it is rejected. The value at risk is the whole heap, not a percentage of it, and the intervention when you catch it early is ordinary: run the aeration fans, turn the grain, move the affected section.

- **Detection** is one CO2 sensor in the headspace or the aeration duct, alongside the temperature cable you already have.
- **The response is cheap and immediate**, which is what makes early detection worth anything at all. Aeration on a rising CO2 reading is a fan running for a few hours.
- **The record matters commercially.** A buyer's rejection is much harder to argue with when you have no storage history, and much easier when you can show the conditions the crop was held in.
- **Humidity tells you to expect trouble; CO2 tells you it has started.** They are worth having together, and the pair is what turns a guess into a decision.

## What to measure if you store grain
